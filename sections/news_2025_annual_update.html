DCENT-I: 2025 Was the Second-Warmest Year on Record

Global temperatures remained exceptionally high, with 2024 remaining the hottest year on record and 2023 and 2025 effectively tied for second

Jan. 14, 2026

The infilled DCENT global surface temperature dataset indicates that 2025 was the second-warmest year on record, at 1.43°C above the pre-industrial level (Fig. 1). Global mean surface temperature (GMST) in 2025 was essentially unchanged from 2023 (within 0.01°C) but 0.10°C cooler than 2024, which remains the warmest year in the instrumental record. Together, 2023–2025 form the warmest three-year period in the DCENT-I record, averaging 1.47°C above pre-industrial — increasing the likelihood of exceeding the Paris Agreement’s 1.5°C warming limit.

Anomalies are reported relative to a common 1850-1900 pre-industrial baseline, estimated by averaging DCENT-I, HadCRUT5, NOAA, Berkeley Earth, and CMST. Despite high consistency with the other products in recent decades, DCENT-I applies more comprehensive adjustments for historical biases in both land air temperatures and sea-surface temperatures and yields the coldest baseline. If anomalies were instead referenced to DCENT-I’s own baseline estimate, the colder baseline would lead to a greater inferred warming of 1.53°C, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.40–1.62°C.

Fig. 1 | Global Mean Surface Temperature. (Left) Annual mean GMST anomalies (°C) relative to the 1850–1900 pre-industrial baseline for DCENT-I (red; shading indicates the 95% CI), HadCRUT5 (blue), NOAA Global Temperature v6 (green), GISTEMPv4 (cyan), Berkeley Earth (yellow), China Merged Surface Temperature (CMST, purple), and ERA5 (pink). Anomalies are first computed relative to each product’s 1981–2010 mean, then aligned using a common offset: the mean 1850–1900 minus 1981–2010 difference across products (0.69°C). (Right) Monthly DCENT GMST anomalies, computed using an analogous monthly offset method.

On a monthly basis, 2025 GMST anomalies exceeded 1.5°C from January to April, then remained below 1.5°C for the rest of the year. The slight cooling relative to 2024 is consistent with weak La Niña–like conditions in the eastern equatorial Pacific, superimposed on the long-term warming trend (Fig. 2).

Outside the equatorial Pacific, much of the tropics were also slightly cooler in 2025 than in 2023 and 2024 (Fig. 2). This cooling was partly offset by pronounced extratropical warming, leaving large areas still markedly warmer than recent climatology. DCENT-I suggests that, in 2025, about 12% of the global surface experienced the highest local annual temperature since 1850, including parts of Northern Eurasia, the Southwestern U.S., Antarctica, the North Pacific, and the Arctic. Over the past three years nearly half the Earth’s surface has experienced its highest annual average temperature.

This sustained warmth reflects the continued dominance of human-driven warming, even as year-to-year variability modulates the exact ranking of individual years.

The DCENT-I product is jointly developed by the University of Southampton, the National Oceanography Centre, Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution and Harvard University. These findings are released today as part of a coordinated annual update with WMO, NASA, NOAA, the UK Met Office, Berkeley Earth, ECMWF, and CMST. This is a rapid update, and the full update and data release for 2025 will follow once the source data are more complete in a few weeks in February.
